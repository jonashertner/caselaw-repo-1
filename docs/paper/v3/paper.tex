\begin{abstract}
We present \textbf{OpenCaseLaw}, an open, continuously-refreshed corpus of \textbf{971{,}992} Swiss court decisions across \textbf{108 courts} spanning \textbf{26 Swiss cantons, the federal jurisdiction, and the Swiss-relevant subset of the European Court of Human Rights} (28 jurisdictional layers), in German, French, and Italian, paired with a citation graph of \textbf{8.65M} distinct outgoing references (\textbf{8.09M} resolved, 93.5\,\% coverage) and a statute graph of \textbf{11.26M} edges over \textbf{283{,}119} distinct provisions. The corpus is bridged to \textbf{5{,}516} federal and \textbf{15{,}722} cantonal laws (\textbf{753{,}842} articles in total), \textbf{362} CC-BY scholarly commentaries, and \textbf{5{,}292} Federal Council Botschaften (Materialien, DE/FR/IT-parallel, with \textbf{381{,}711} full-text paragraphs and \textbf{8{,}124} article-anchored links). The infrastructure is served through a deployed MCP/REST endpoint at \texttt{mcp.opencaselaw.ch} (33 tools), a Word-add-in client, and a public dashboard. Snapshot: 2026-05-13. We use the resource to introduce three contributions:
(i)~a released corpus and knowledge graph with documented provenance, licensing, and a four-layer dataset-health framework (63 codified checks, drift detection, and a publish-gate that blocks production updates on critical regression);
(ii)~a \emph{regeste-derived cross-lingual retrieval diagnostic} with parallel keyword queries in DE/FR/IT against 50 highly-cited decisions. Because each query is extracted from its target case's own multilingual regeste, the absolute scores are an upper bound on realistic legal-research retrieval; we therefore report per-direction asymmetries as the interpretable signal (Sec.~\ref{sec:crosslang}, Sec.~\ref{sec:limit});
(iii)~a deployed five-rail closing audit that checks named legal-RAG error classes deterministically (case-citation existence, statute resolution, verbatim-quote source matching, decision-date sanity) plus an opt-in proposition-grounding judge, with per-class metrics and explicit calibration limits (Sec.~\ref{sec:audit}, Sec.~\ref{sec:limit}). We do \emph{not} claim a general hallucination-rate reduction.
Released artifacts (corpus packaging, citation/statute graphs, benchmark) follow record-level upstream terms: code is MIT; original-source decisions are official acts of Swiss authorities and inherit their public-domain status; commentaries retain CC-BY-4.0 / CC-BY-SA-4.0; the entscheidsuche.ch mirror is CC-BY-4.0. A public dashboard tracks live dataset health.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Legal artificial intelligence has converged on retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) as the standard architecture for question answering over jurisprudence. Yet recent measurement studies report fabrication rates of 58--88\,\% on general-purpose LLMs (ChatGPT-4 to Llama-2 range)~\cite{dahl2024} and 17--33\,\% on commercial legal-RAG products~\cite{magesh2024}. The mitigation literature is methodologically sophisticated but concentrated on \emph{English-language, single-jurisdiction} settings where retrieval substrates and ground-truth citation graphs are limited.

Switzerland is the most multilingual major civil-law jurisdiction. The authoritative version of a leading Swiss federal decision may be in German, French, or Italian, and citations cross language boundaries: in our resolved citation graph, \textbf{34.0\,\% of resolved citations are cross-lingual} (Table~\ref{tab:cross_lang_citation}). Building a credible RAG system for Swiss law therefore requires (a)~a corpus that spans the federal--cantonal landscape across all three languages, (b)~a citation and statute graph that can verify generated claims, and (c)~evaluation that measures cross-lingual retrieval as a first-class concern.

We present \textbf{OpenCaseLaw}, an open infrastructure that fills these gaps. We frame this work as a \emph{resource paper with diagnostic evaluations}: the corpus, citation graph, and statute graph are the contribution; the cross-lingual numbers and the audit pipeline are diagnostics that characterise specific failure modes, not general claims about retrieval quality or hallucination mitigation.

\paragraph{C1 --- Released corpus and knowledge graph.} We release an open Swiss legal knowledge graph built from a continuously refreshed corpus of court decisions, resolved case-to-case citations, statute references, legislative materials (Federal Council Botschaften), and open commentaries. The resource covers federal, cantonal, and Swiss-relevant ECHR decisions in German, French, and Italian. We document provenance, licensing per record category, and operate a four-layer dataset-health framework that codifies 63 invariants with drift detection and a publish-gate that blocks production updates on critical regression (Section~\ref{sec:qc}).

\paragraph{C2 --- Regeste-derived cross-lingual retrieval diagnostic.} We provide a parallel-query set in DE/FR/IT against 50 highly-cited Swiss decisions, with each query extracted from the target case's own official regeste. The resulting MRR@10$=$0.630 is an \emph{upper bound} on realistic retrieval performance because the query and target share construction vocabulary; we therefore foreground the directional asymmetries (IT$\to$DE$=$0.776 vs.\ DE$\to$FR$=$0.333) rather than the absolute headline (Section~\ref{sec:crosslang}, Section~\ref{sec:limit}).

\paragraph{C3 --- Deterministic legal-RAG audit layer.} We deploy a five-rail closing audit (Section~\ref{sec:audit}) that checks specific legal-RAG error classes: case-citation existence (R1), statute resolution (R2), verbatim-quote source matching (R3), decision-date sanity (R4), and proposition grounding (R5, judge-mediated, advisory). We report per-rail metrics on the 30-question Swiss Legal RAG bench under a prior-only condition (R1 unresolvable-citation rate, R5 false-positive rate of 41.7\,\%), and we do \emph{not} claim a general hallucination-rate reduction.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Open legal corpora.} The Caselaw Access Project~\cite{cap2018} releases 6.7M US decisions but does not publish a citation graph or maintain a retrieval system. The \emph{Open Legal Data} platform~\cite{ostendorff2020} releases German court decisions with a citation network but no statute graph and no multilingual coverage; \emph{LeCNet}~\cite{lecnet2025} introduces an Indian-judicial-context citation-network benchmark (26\,k case-judgment nodes) for missing-citation recommendation. \emph{Pile of Law}~\cite{pile2022} and \emph{MultiLegalPile}~\cite{niklaus2023} are pretraining corpora rather than retrieval substrates. Within Swiss legal NLP, \emph{Swiss-Judgment-Prediction}~\cite{niklaus2021} is a binary outcome-prediction task; the \emph{One Law, Many Languages} benchmark~\cite{stern2024} bundles diverse Swiss-legal NLP tasks (document-to-document IR, court-view generation, leading-decision summarisation, citation extraction, classification) across five languages; \emph{Swiss Landmark Decisions Summarisation}~\cite{rolshoven2025} is a 20\,k-decision dataset where each ruling is paired with headnotes in all three official languages (DE/FR/IT) for cross-lingual summarisation. All three target narrow downstream NLP tasks rather than the underlying retrieval substrate; we release the corpus + citation graph + statute graph + Materialien layer that such tasks can be built on top of. The closest Swiss-specific comparator is the community project \emph{entscheidsuche.ch}~\cite{entscheidsuche}, which aggregates raw HTML/PDF feeds from cantonal and federal court portals into a unified harvesting layer (CC BY 4.0). entscheidsuche.ch is in fact one of our upstream sources --- many of our cantonal scrapers federate via its JSON feeds. Critically, however, entscheidsuche.ch provides no citation graph, no statute mirror, no Materialien layer, no retrieval API, and no cross-court normalisation, dedup, or multilingual joins; we build all of these on top.

\paragraph{Legal-RAG benchmarks.} \emph{LegalBench-RAG}~\cite{pipitone2024} introduces retrieval evaluation for US legal RAG with $\sim$79M characters of human-annotated source material. \emph{Butler\,\&\,Butler 2026}~\cite{butler2026} adds end-to-end correctness$\times$groundedness$\times$retrieval-accuracy decomposition on Victorian criminal law. \emph{Bar Exam QA / Housing Statute QA}~\cite{zheng2025} are US-focused legal-research QA benchmarks. None of these benchmarks measure cross-lingual retrieval where the target authoritative version may be in a different language than the query.

\paragraph{Hallucination measurement and mitigation.} \emph{Dahl et al.\ 2024}~\cite{dahl2024} and \emph{Magesh et al.\ 2024}~\cite{magesh2024} establish hallucination rates on general LLMs and commercial legal-RAG. \emph{Self-RAG}~\cite{asai2023} embeds critique directly in the generator via model-emitted reflection tokens, controlling retrieval and self-evaluation inline. \emph{CRAG}~\cite{yan2024} adds a lightweight retrieval evaluator that scores retrieved documents and triggers corrective web search when confidence is low. We instead decompose verification into per-error-class deterministic rails (case-citation existence, statute resolution, verbatim-quote source matching, decision-date sanity) plus an opt-in proposition-grounding judge.

\paragraph{Positioning.} To our knowledge, OpenCaseLaw is the first open Swiss legal resource that bundles cross-court case-law aggregation, resolved case-to-case citation links, statute references, legislative-material (Botschaft) links, scholarly commentary anchors, and a public retrieval API in a single release. Each component individually has prior art (cited above); the contribution is the integrated graph, the multilingual scope, and the operational health framework around it. The cross-lingual diagnostic and the audit layer accompany the resource but are not the headline claim.

\section{Resource: corpus}\label{sec:corpus}

\input{tables/corpus_overview.tex}

The OpenCaseLaw corpus comprises \textbf{971{,}992 decisions} from \textbf{108 distinct courts} across \textbf{26 Swiss cantons plus federal and ECHR jurisdictions} (28 jurisdictional layers). The federal Supreme Court (BGer) is refreshed hourly (Mon--Fri 05:00--16:00 UTC) via a dedicated Neuheiten poller; all other federations (BVGer, BStGer, BPatGer, regulatory authorities, cantonal portals, ECHR-Swiss-subset, historical archives) are refreshed by a nightly publish at 03:00 UTC. Coverage spans \textbf{1875--2026} and three official languages: German (449{,}575 / 46.3\,\%), French (441{,}158 / 45.4\,\%), Italian (80{,}704 / 8.3\,\%). The federation aggregates source-specific scrapers and adapters ranging from federal courts (BGer, BVGer, BStGer, BPatGer) to per-canton portals to the European Court of Human Rights (Swiss subset; see below), historical archives (BGE 1875--1953, MKG 1915--2025), and regulatory authorities (FINMA, WEKO, ED\"OB, UBI, ELCom, PostCom, ComCom).

\paragraph{ECHR Swiss subset.} The Swiss-relevant subset of European Court of Human Rights jurisprudence (\textbf{2{,}788 decisions}: 1{,}153 chamber + 93 grand-chamber + 230 committee + 835 HUDOC entries + 477 BGE-EGMR mirror) is integrated as a first-class federation. The set is refreshed daily; on the snapshot date the most recent ingest carried a same-day Article 11 violation against Switzerland (application 30781/22, decided 2026-05-07). The subset matters because \textbf{Article 6~ECHR} is the second-most-cited statutory provision in our graph (after federal-court procedure), and the official versions of ECHR-Switzerland judgments are typically French and English, requiring a multilingual retrieval substrate.

\paragraph{Completeness.} Some source portals impose access restrictions (geographic, rate, or contractual). We document affected sources in the per-source coverage report, do not claim full coverage where access is restricted, and use only legally authorized collection methods. For closed historical archives where contemporaneous collection is no longer possible, we recover from publicly archived snapshots (e.g.\ Wayback Machine; $\sim$903 BL ALS judgments).

\paragraph{Licensing.} Court decisions in Switzerland are official published acts under URG/CopA Art.~5 (excluded from copyright); the corpus packaging and added metadata are released CC0 to the extent rights subsist. Upstream sources retain their own terms: scholarly commentaries are CC-BY-4.0 (OnlineKommentar.ch) and CC-BY-SA-4.0 (OpenLegalCommentary.ch); the cantonal-level mirror entscheidsuche.ch~\cite{entscheidsuche} which we federate as one input source is CC-BY-4.0. Per-record license categories, the URG Art.~5 posture, the FADP (Swiss data protection) treatment of personal data, and the takedown procedure are documented in Appendix~\ref{app:licensing}. Decision IDs are stable across daily refreshes barring documented corrections (see \S\ref{sec:qc} case studies).

\input{tables/citation_graph.tex}

\paragraph{Citation graph.} We extract citation edges by full-text scanning every decision in the corpus (\textbf{971{,}852} decisions in the reference-graph snapshot; the FTS5 search index carries 140 more decisions ingested between graph rebuild and FTS5 swap), recovering \textbf{8{,}649{,}879 distinct outgoing references} of which \textbf{8{,}089{,}112 (93.5\,\%)} resolve to a target decision in the corpus. Resolution proceeds in three exact-match passes (general docket-normalisation; BGE/ATF/DTF prefix-form; bare BGE numeric form), augmented by a pin-cite-aware fallback for Swiss legal citations such as \emph{BGE 125 V 352} that pinpoint into the body of a case starting at \emph{BGE 125 V 351}. The pin-cite resolver matches each unresolved BGE-form reference to the nearest preceding first-page in the same volume\,$+$\,division within 30 pages, contributing \textbf{652{,}310} additional matches at a $-$0.10 confidence discount versus exact matches. The remaining 560{,}767 unresolved pairs (6.5\,\%) are dominated by cantonal or lower-court references genuinely outside our current coverage; the residual is BGE volumes we do not hold and malformed extraction artefacts (see \texttt{benchmarks/citation\_resolution\_analysis.py}). The deployed 93.5\,\% resolution is an 18.3\,pp lift over the prior release's 75.2\,\%, recovered by fixing two implementation bugs: docket-normalisation drift in 2007--2009 \emph{Bundesgericht} dockets stored with a space (\texttt{8C 862\_2008}) versus the underscore form (\texttt{8C\_862\_2008}) emitted by the citation extractor, and the pin-cite mismatch above.

The citation graph is heavy-tailed (Table~\ref{tab:citation_graph}): 47 decisions are cited 10{,}000+ times, 952 are cited 1{,}000--9{,}999 times, and the rest follow a power law. The single most-cited decision is BGE 125 V 351 (85{,}108 incoming citations), the foundational case on the evidentiary weight of party-commissioned medical reports in social-insurance litigation.

\paragraph{Precision proxies.} The 93.5\,\% resolution number above is a \emph{coverage} metric --- the share of extracted citation tokens that match a corpus decision --- and does not by itself bound precision. A per-stratum manual precision audit (400 stratified samples, over-sampling the pin-cite fallback; harness shipped at \path{benchmarks/citation_precision_audit.py}) is deferred to a multi-annotator v2.0 effort. In its place we report three automated proxies (Table~\ref{tab:precision_proxies}) that bound the worst case: every counted violation is a guaranteed false positive. \emph{Date sanity} (target decision must be decided before source) passes 98.47\,\% over all 8.10M resolved pairs with both dates known --- a hard lower bound on overall precision. The pin-cite stratum scores lowest (97.35\,\%), consistent with its heuristic nature; the exact-match strata (\texttt{bge\_bare}, \texttt{bge\_norm}) sit above 99.2\,\%. \emph{Self-citation} is zero across the full graph. \emph{Confidence percentiles} per match\_type are reported alongside as a descriptive signal. These proxies are necessary but not sufficient; full precision intervals from human adjudication remain a v2.0 deliverable.

\paragraph{Cross-lingual citation.} Table~\ref{tab:cross_lang_citation} reports the language-by-language citation matrix. \textbf{34.0\,\% of resolved citations with identified source and target language are cross-lingual}: French decisions cite German decisions \textbf{1.94M} times (FR$\to$DE dominates the off-diagonal), German decisions cite French decisions \textbf{0.46M} times, and Italian decisions cite German decisions \textbf{0.17M} times. The asymmetry --- DE$\leftrightarrow$FR is bidirectional but FR$\to$DE outweighs DE$\to$FR by 4.2$\times$, while IT$\to$DE/FR (0.24M combined) dominates IT$\to$IT (46{,}960) by $\sim$5$\times$ --- reflects practitioners' practical reliance on German jurisprudence as binding precedent in mixed-language litigation.

\paragraph{Statute graph.} We resolve \textbf{11{,}261{,}717 decision-to-statute edges} touching \textbf{283{,}119 distinct provisions}. The statute layer is grounded in two parallel mirrors: a federal layer of \textbf{5{,}516 SR-numbered Fedlex laws} (400{,}405 article rows summed across the parallel DE/FR/IT versions; $\sim$133\,k articles per language) and a cantonal layer of \textbf{15{,}722 laws} (353{,}437 articles) covering all 26 cantons via direct portal scraping (LexWork, 18 cantons; SIL, 2 cantons; ZH OpenData, ZH; TI~RL, TI), with LexFind PDF fallback supplementing 4 cantons for laws missing from their primary portals. Top-referenced provisions (Table~\ref{tab:top_statutes}, after alias canonicalisation across 20 major DE/FR/IT statute abbreviation pairs) are dominated by federal-court procedural code: \emph{BGG/LTF} Art.~100 (\textbf{205{,}725} decisions), Art.~42 (\textbf{201{,}897}, motivation requirement), and Art.~113 (\textbf{122{,}514}, deadline). Beyond procedural code, \emph{ATSG/LPGA} Art.~61 (social-insurance procedure, 85{,}207) and \emph{BV/Cst./Cost.} Art.~29 (procedural fundamental rights, 79{,}115) lead the non-BGG/LTF substantive provisions. The alias canonicalisation is lexical only; temporal validity --- mapping a 1998 \emph{OG} reference (the pre-2007 Federal Justice Act, superseded by BGG/LTF) to its current SR location, or distinguishing repealed-vs-current article versions --- is a v2.0 deliverable, not addressed in this release.

\paragraph{Materialien graph (Federal Council Botschaften).} A distinguishing feature of Swiss civil-law interpretation is the \emph{teleological} reading of statutes via \emph{Materialien} --- legislative-history documents (Federal Council messages, parliamentary debates) that record legislative intent. We discover Materialien via the Fedlex JOLUX SPARQL endpoint (\texttt{typeDocument} = 23) and ingest them as \textbf{5{,}292 Botschaft documents} (ranging 2003--2026, with DE/FR/IT-parallel coverage), parsed at paragraph granularity (\textbf{381{,}711 paragraphs}) from Akoma Ntoso XML or PDF fallback. We anchor paragraphs to specific \texttt{(SR, article)} pairs by traversing the parliament-chain (\texttt{parliamentDraftId} $\to$ enacted \texttt{oc} $\to$ codified \texttt{cc} $\to$ SR), yielding \textbf{8{,}124 article-to-Botschaft links} (DE 2{,}798, FR 2{,}742, IT 2{,}584). The Materialien layer is queryable via \texttt{search\_materialien} / \texttt{get\_materialien} MCP tools and surfaces verbatim legislative-intent text under any cited statute article. To our knowledge this is the first open, machine-queryable bridge between the Swiss case-law corpus and the Botschaften that a court would consult in interpretation.

\section{Infrastructure}\label{sec:infra}

The corpus, graph, statutes, and Materialien are served through four production surfaces:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{MCP server} (\texttt{mcp.opencaselaw.ch/sse}, public SSE). 33 public tools partitioned into four families: retrieval over cases, statutes, commentaries, Materialien, and practice notes; analysis over citations, appeal chains, trends, doctrine, article history, regestes, case briefs, and court statistics; generation of exam questions and mock decisions; and verification through claim support, attestations, and citation formatting. Two additional maintenance tools are local-only and hidden by the \texttt{REMOTE\_MODE} filter on the public deployment.
\item \textbf{REST API} (FastAPI; export endpoints \texttt{.docx / .pdf / .bib / .ris} per decision; OpenAPI spec at \texttt{/api/openapi.copilot.json} curated for Microsoft Copilot Studio adoption).
\item \textbf{Word add-in} (\texttt{opencaselaw.ch/word}, public). Lawyer-facing client that surfaces the verification rails as inline citations, with structural client+server PII redaction at boundary.
\item \textbf{Public dashboards} at \url{https://opencaselaw.ch} (corpus stats, multilingual landing) and \url{https://opencaselaw.ch/quality.html} (live dataset health).
\end{itemize}

The retrieval substrate is a SQLite FTS5 index ($\sim$63\,GB on disk). The nightly refresh uses an \emph{atomic-rebuild} pattern: the new index is constructed in a sibling \texttt{.db.tmp} file, then promoted via \texttt{os.replace}; live readers (4 uvicorn workers behind nginx) are never interrupted.

\paragraph{Liveness signal.} The deployed MCP endpoint is exercised by external clients (multiple coding-agent and search-engine integrations identified by request user-agent over a four-week window). We summarise aggregate counts in Appendix~\ref{app:adoption} and emphasise: adoption is descriptive (the resource is in use), not an evaluation claim about quality. We do not log query payloads.

\paragraph{Traffic pattern across courts.} Tier-1 server logs (which include both bot indexers and human visitors) show that \emph{cantonal} jurisprudence receives more decision-page hits than federal jurisprudence: hits to the \emph{Tribunal civil de Gen\`eve} (\texttt{ge\_gerichte}, 252.6\,k) exceed those to the federal \emph{Bundesgericht} (\texttt{bger}, 209.5\,k); Vaud (\texttt{vd\_findinfo} + \texttt{vd\_gerichte}, 100.9\,k combined), Z\"urich (\texttt{zh\_obergericht}, 35.6\,k), Ticino (\texttt{ti\_gerichte}, 30.9\,k), and St.~Gallen (\texttt{sg\_publikationen}, 36.3\,k) likewise pull substantial traffic. The same rank-ordering persists after a tier-3 bot-vs-browser filter (cantonal $>$ federal in absolute volume), although the bot-stripped totals are lower. The pattern is consistent with a corollary the federation makes empirically testable: cantonal decisions occupy a layer of legal-practice retrieval that a federal-only corpus -- the standard scope of prior open Swiss datasets -- would miss.

\section{Five-rail closing audit}\label{sec:audit}

We deploy a five-rail closing audit on every LLM response generated by the public MCP. Each rail addresses a single named error class: (R1)~case-citation existence, (R2)~statute reference resolution, (R3)~verbatim-quote source matching, (R4)~decision-date sanity, and (R5)~LLM-judge proposition grounding. R1--R4 are deterministic regex+lookup ($\sim$3\,ms total), R5 is judge-mediated ($\sim$2\,s, $\sim$\$0.005). The decomposition by error class lets each rail be tested, ablated, and improved in isolation.

\paragraph{Setup.} We select 50 leading cases from the most-cited subset of the corpus (in-degree range $4{,}412$--$53{,}251$, median $8{,}180$, measured at benchmark-construction time; the citation graph has since grown --- see Table~\ref{tab:citation_graph} and Table~\ref{tab:top_cited} for current values), stratified to at most four cases per legal area to ensure diversity across the 22 covered areas (constitutional, social-insurance, federal-court procedure, criminal, civil-procedure, contract, tax, asylum, and more). All 50 cases lie in the top 0.025\,\% of distinct cited decisions in the corpus by in-degree. For each case we extract one query per language (DE, FR, IT) from terms in that language's official regeste, after dropping article-reference noise (e.g.\ \texttt{Art.\ 6 Abs.\ 1 UVG}) and language-specific stop words. The query construction is keyword-list style, not a free natural-language paraphrase; we discuss the realism implications in Section~\ref{sec:limit}. The dataset is released as \path{benchmarks/swiss_legal_rag_bench/cross_lingual_v1.jsonl} (CC0). Of the 50 cases, 38 are originally German and 12 originally French --- consistent with the corpus distribution among most-cited decisions. No selected case is originally Italian; the absent Italian-target column is therefore a sampling limitation, not a failed retrieval cell, and we treat it as a coverage gap (Section~\ref{sec:limit}). \emph{The benchmark is single-curator; multi-annotator agreement is deferred to v2.0, and we read the absolute scores as an upper bound on realistic retrieval performance (Section~\ref{sec:limit}).}

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:cross_lingual_mrr} reports MRR@10 and Table~\ref{tab:cross_lingual_hit} reports Hit@10 per (Q lang, T lang) cell. Overall MRR@10$=$0.630, Hit@10$=$0.833 (125 of 150 queries place the target in the top 10).

\paragraph{Findings.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Italian queries retrieve German targets best (MRR$=$0.776).} Italian queries outperform German queries on the \emph{same} 38 German targets (DE$\to$DE MRR$=$0.617). One mechanism: Italian regestes carry distinctive shared markers (\emph{DTF}, \emph{cpv.}, \emph{LAINF}) that anchor retrieval, while German queries compete with the broader German subset (449\,k decisions, the largest stratum) and so retrieve more in-language distractors.
\item \textbf{Same-language French is the strongest cell (MRR$=$0.785, Hit@10$=$1.0).} For the 12 French-original leading cases, French queries place the target in the top 10 every time and at rank 1 in 67\,\% of cases.
\item \textbf{German-to-French is the hardest cell (MRR$=$0.333, Hit@10$=$0.333).} German queries struggle to retrieve French-original BGEs because the discriminative German terms in the regeste-derived queries do not appear in the French-language full text and the BM25+RRF baseline does no cross-lingual translation.
\item \textbf{Diagonal vs.\ off-diagonal gap is small.} Weighted across n, same-language diagonal cells (DE$\to$DE n$=$38, FR$\to$FR n$=$12) score MRR$=$0.657; the four off-diagonal cells (n$=$100 total) score MRR$=$0.616. The 0.04 gap shows that cross-lingual retrieval against a multilingual leading-case index is feasible without translation, although the cell-by-cell variance (0.333 to 0.776) is large.
\end{itemize}

\section{Bench: prior-only vs.\ retrieval-augmented}\label{sec:hallu}

We evaluate end-to-end draft quality on a 30-question Swiss Legal RAG bench (\path{benchmarks/swiss_legal_rag_bench/questions.jsonl}; spans 17 substantive areas; languages DE\,{=}\,18, FR\,{=}\,8, IT\,{=}\,4; each question paired with a corpus-anchored reference answer plus annotated statute and where applicable a leading-case \texttt{decision\_id}). We adopt the Butler\,\&\,Butler 2026~\cite{butler2026} correctness $\times$ groundedness $\times$ retrieval-accuracy decomposition (4 outcome classes: \emph{correct} / \emph{hallucination} / \emph{retrieval} / \emph{reasoning}). Generator and judges are Claude Sonnet 4.6; retriever is the live MCP \texttt{/api/decisions} endpoint with top-K\,{=}\,5 and annotated statute text injected. Reproducible via \path{benchmarks/swiss_legal_rag_bench/evaluate.py}.

\input{tables/audit_bench_results.tex}

\paragraph{Conditions and results.} Table~\ref{tab:audit_bench_results} reports both conditions side-by-side. The \emph{prior-only} condition skips retrieval entirely: the generator answers from training prior, with no passages provided. The \emph{RAG-augmented} condition runs the full pipeline (retrieve top-5 cases + inject annotated statute text + answer constrained to retrieved passages).

\paragraph{Counter-intuitive finding.} The prior-only condition scores higher on correctness (86.7\,\%, 26/30) than the retrieval-augmented condition (63.3\,\%, 19/30). The gap is driven not by hallucinations --- groundedness in the RAG-augmented condition is 93.3\,\% (28/30) --- but by retrieval failures: only 53.3\,\% (16/30) of questions had the annotated leading case in the top-5 retrieved set, and the strict ``answer using ONLY the retrieved passages'' prompt blocks the generator from falling back to its (substantial) prior knowledge of Swiss federal law. Of the 13 RAG-aug failures, 9 are \emph{retrieval} class (the model honestly used what was retrieved but the right case wasn't in it), 2 are \emph{reasoning} (right context, wrong conclusion), and only 2 are true \emph{hallucination} (claimed something not in the passages).

\paragraph{Interpretation.} For a model with Sonnet 4.6's prior coverage of Swiss federal law, naive RAG-augmentation is a net regression on this bench unless one of (a) retrieval precision exceeds the generator's prior accuracy, or (b) the prompt allows fallback to prior knowledge when retrieval comes back empty-handed. We do \emph{not} claim a general hallucination-rate result. The 13.3\,\% prior-only hallucination rate (4/30, all in the DE stratum) is below commercial-legal-RAG numbers reported by Magesh et al.~\cite{magesh2024} (17--33\,\%) and far below the general-LLM range from Dahl et al.~\cite{dahl2024} (58--88\,\%), but the conditions, jurisdictions, models, and grading criteria all differ; we cite to bracket the literature, not as a like-for-like comparison.

\paragraph{The five-rail audit (Section~\ref{sec:audit}) is condition-agnostic} --- the deterministic rails R1--R4 make no assumption about whether a draft was produced with or without retrieval, so the same deployment audits both pipelines. Rail-specific empirical numbers (e.g.\ R1 unresolvable-citation rate, R5 judge false-positive rate) are reported with the audit-pipeline description in Section~\ref{sec:audit}; we do not duplicate them here.

\paragraph{Retrieval-augmented condition.} A side-by-side retrieval-augmented re-run is the highest-priority follow-on for v1.1. We expect retrieval to substantially reduce R1 fabrication-existence flags (the system retrieves real authorities rather than relying on prior); the open question is whether judge-rail false-positive rate increases (more authorities to ground against) or decreases (clearer source pool). We commit to publish the retrieval-augmented numbers as a versioned update on the benchmark's Hugging Face page rather than holding the resource paper's release on it.

\section{Dataset health and drift}\label{sec:qc}

OpenCaseLaw operates a four-layer quality framework:
\begin{description}
\item[L1 Function tests] (575 pytest cases across 58 modules, every CI push, $\sim$90\,s).
\item[L2 Pipeline guards] (5 codified \texttt{\_normalize\_*} passes in \texttt{build\_fts5.py}, every nightly rebuild).
\item[L3 Dataset audit] (63 \texttt{check\_*} functions across 17 axes: schema, dates, dockets, courts, languages, regeste coverage, URL integrity, duplicates, text quality, structure, cross-DB consistency, citation graph, statute graph, floors, per-court drift, exports round-trip, MCP-tool round-trip).
\item[L4 Production smoke] (every 5\,min via systemd timer; 6 endpoint probes; $p_{95}<2$\,s).
\end{description}

A dataset-audit run that detects a critical regression \emph{blocks the nightly publish}: the orchestrator (\texttt{publish.py}) refuses to advance to the git-push step and instead emits an urgent ntfy alert. Critical conditions include a total-count cliff $>$1\,\%, schema break, EGMR re-regression, court not in registry, future date count $>$50, cross-DB row-count mismatch $>$0.5\,\%. Warning conditions (per-court drift $>$10\,\% from 7-day median, OCR-quality regression, citation-graph edge drop $>$5\,\%) emit alerts but do not block publish.

Operationally, production changes are governed by a committed decision-rules document (\texttt{docs/decision\_rules.md}) that requires runbooks for production changes, manual operator review before any public rollback, Saturday-only schema changes, and measurable dashboard backing for public claims.

\section{Discussion and generalisation}\label{sec:disc}

The cross-lingual results have a clear takeaway for legal-RAG practitioners: \emph{retrieval quality is asymmetric across languages and direction}. A naive deployment that only validates same-language MRR will overestimate user-facing quality in mixed-language jurisdictions. Our public benchmark allows quantitative pre-deployment audits of cross-lingual performance.

The verification framework generalises beyond Switzerland. Each rail of the closing audit is corpus-agnostic given (a) an enumerable decision corpus, (b) a statute mirror, and (c) an extraction grammar for the local citation conventions. We sketch an English-law application in Appendix~\ref{app:english}. The Materialien layer is the most directly Switzerland-specific contribution: jurisdictions with comparable legislative-history doctrine (Germany's \emph{Gesetzgebungsmaterialien}, Canada's \emph{Hansard}, the United States' \emph{legislative history}) could replicate the parliament-chain SPARQL traversal pattern over their own gazette ontologies.

The early-adoption signal --- a Swiss-French law firm and a cantonal-government legal department actively querying the deployed MCP within weeks of soft launch --- suggests that the resource fills a deployment-blocker gap rather than only a research-evaluation gap. The corollary risk: any infrastructure regression now affects production legal research, which motivates the four-layer QC framework (Section~\ref{sec:qc}) as a non-negotiable accompaniment to release.

\section{Limitations and ethics}\label{sec:limit}

\paragraph{Benchmark scope.} The cross-lingual question set is single-curator and limited to 50 cases (150 trials). It is anchored on the citation graph (target decisions are objectively-ranked leading cases) but the queries themselves are programmatic keyword extractions from the corresponding language's regeste, not free legal-research paraphrases. v2.0 of the benchmark will recruit multi-annotator panels to reach inter-annotator agreement.

\paragraph{Query--target coupling (upper-bound caveat).} Because each query is derived from the same case's official multilingual regeste, the query and target share distinctive vocabulary the retrieval system can latch onto. A real legal-research query, written without sight of the regeste, may not contain those discriminators. We therefore read the absolute MRR/Hit@10 numbers as an \emph{upper bound} on realistic retrieval performance, and the cross-cell ranking (which cells are easier or harder, by how much) as the more reliable signal. v2.0 will replace regeste-derived queries with held-out, lawyer-authored research questions.

\paragraph{Italian-target coverage.} No top-50 case is originally Italian (top-50 is dominated by social-insurance and procedural BGEs that originate in the German-language stream). This is consistent with the corpus distribution among most-cited decisions but is a coverage gap; v2.0 will explicitly include Italian-original cases sampled at lower citation centrality.

\paragraph{Privacy.} Court decisions in Switzerland are public acts. Pseudonymisation practice varies across cantons and even across the same court's chambers; the corpus inherits whatever pseudonymisation the source decision carries. We do not de-pseudonymise, and we also do not re-pseudonymise material that the issuing court already published un-pseudonymised. We support take-down requests through the contact channel listed on the public dashboard. The Pro features of the Word add-in apply client-side structural PII redaction before any LLM round-trip; the full FADP posture and takedown SLA are documented in Appendix~\ref{app:licensing}.

\paragraph{Calibration.} The grounding-judge rail (R5) is a judge-mediated LLM call; we report a 41.7\,\% false-positive rate on correct drafts (the figure cited consistently in Section~\ref{sec:audit} and \path{sections/audit_pipeline.tex}). Full-binary deployment would over-flag; we recommend the rail be used in advisory mode pending v2.0 calibration work.
